\section{PLI for normal-mode splitting coefficients}
\label{appendix:pli-splitting-functions}

This appendix derives the probabilistic linear inference (PLI) problem associated
with normal-mode splitting coefficients.  The physical perturbations are
naturally three-dimensional fields, while discontinuity perturbations are
naturally two-dimensional fields on spherical interfaces.  The splitting-function
formalism does not change this fact.  Instead, it uses the spherical symmetry of
the reference Earth to analyze these fields in spherical harmonics.

Thus the spherical-harmonic representation should be understood as a
symmetry-adapted representation of a higher-dimensional function-space problem,
not as an upfront discretization of the Earth model.  The bulk
spherical-harmonic coefficients remain functions of radius.

\subsection{Natural three-dimensional and surface model spaces}

Let
\[
    \xi \in \mathbb{S}^2
\]
denote angular position on the unit sphere, and let \(r\in(0,R)\) denote
radius.  The bulk perturbations are
\[
    \delta v_p(r,\xi),\qquad
    \delta v_s(r,\xi),\qquad
    \delta\rho(r,\xi),
\]
while the perturbation of interface \(\Sigma_\beta\) is a function
\[
    \delta\Sigma_\beta(\xi),
    \qquad
    \beta=1,\dots,N_\Sigma .
\]
Before spherical-harmonic analysis, the natural model space is therefore
\begin{equation}
    \modelspace_{\mathrm{3D}}
    =
    \modelspace^{v_p}_{\Omega}
    \oplus
    \modelspace^{v_s}_{\Omega}
    \oplus
    \modelspace^{\rho}_{\Omega}
    \oplus
    \bigoplus_{\beta=1}^{N_\Sigma}
    \modelspace^{\Sigma_\beta},
    \label{eq:app_3d_model_space}
\end{equation}
where the bulk spaces contain functions on \((0,R)\times \mathbb{S}^2\), and
\(\modelspace^{\Sigma_\beta}\) contains functions on \(\mathbb{S}^2\).

The inner products used after spherical-harmonic analysis are induced from
these full spaces.  For a bulk parameter \(p\in\{v_p,v_s,\rho\}\), consider a
rotationally invariant weighted inner product
\begin{equation}
    \left(f,g\right)_{\Omega,p}
    =
    \int_0^R
    \int_{\mathbb{S}^2}
    w_p(r)\,
    f(r,\xi)\,
    g(r,\xi)
    \,d\xi\,dr,
    \label{eq:app_3d_bulk_inner_product}
\end{equation}
where \(w_p(r)>0\) is a radial weight.  For example, the standard volume
\(L^2\) geometry corresponds to \(w_p(r)=r^2\), up to any additional physical
scaling.

For interface perturbations, a natural surface inner product is
\begin{equation}
    \left(\delta\Sigma_\beta,\delta\widetilde\Sigma_\beta\right)_{\Sigma_\beta}
    =
    q_\beta
    \int_{\mathbb{S}^2}
    \delta\Sigma_\beta(\xi)\,
    \delta\widetilde\Sigma_\beta(\xi)
    \,d\xi,
    \label{eq:app_surface_inner_product}
\end{equation}
where \(q_\beta>0\) is an optional surface weight.  In the simplest case,
\(q_\beta=1\).

\subsection{Spherical-harmonic analysis and synthesis}

Let \(\{Y_s^t\}\) be an orthonormal spherical-harmonic basis of \(L^2(\mathbb{S}^2)\).
Let
\[
    \mathcal I
    =
    \{(s,t):0\le s\le s_{\max},\ -s\le t\le s\}
\]
denote the retained set of angular indices.

For a bulk field \(f(r,\xi)\), define the spherical-harmonic analysis operator
\[
    \Pi_{\mathcal I}^{\Omega}:
    \modelspace^{p}_{\Omega}
    \to
    \bigoplus_{(s,t)\in\mathcal I}
    \modelspace^{p}_{st}
\]
by
\begin{equation}
    [\Pi_{\mathcal I}^{\Omega}f]_{st}(r)
    =
    f_{st}(r)
    =
    \int_{\mathbb{S}^2}
    f(r,\xi)\,
    \overline{Y_s^t(\xi)}
    \,d\xi .
    \label{eq:app_bulk_analysis}
\end{equation}
The corresponding synthesis operator is
\[
    \pi_{\mathcal I}^{\Omega}:
    \bigoplus_{(s,t)\in\mathcal I}
    \modelspace^{p}_{st}
    \to
    \modelspace^{p}_{\Omega},
\]
with
\begin{equation}
    [\pi_{\mathcal I}^{\Omega}\{f_{st}\}](r,\xi)
    =
    \sum_{(s,t)\in\mathcal I}
    f_{st}(r)\,
    Y_s^t(\xi).
    \label{eq:app_bulk_synthesis}
\end{equation}
Thus \(\pi_{\mathcal I}^{\Omega}\Pi_{\mathcal I}^{\Omega}\) is the orthogonal
projection onto the retained spherical-harmonic subspace.

Similarly, for an interface perturbation \(\delta\Sigma_\beta(\xi)\), define
\begin{equation}
    [\Pi_{\mathcal I}^{\Sigma}\delta\Sigma_\beta]_{st}
    =
    \delta\Sigma_{\beta,st}
    =
    \int_{\mathbb{S}^2}
    \delta\Sigma_\beta(\xi)\,
    \overline{Y_s^t(\xi)}
    \,d\xi,
    \label{eq:app_surface_analysis}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    [\pi_{\mathcal I}^{\Sigma}\{\delta\Sigma_{\beta,st}\}](\xi)
    =
    \sum_{(s,t)\in\mathcal I}
    \delta\Sigma_{\beta,st}
    Y_s^t(\xi).
    \label{eq:app_surface_synthesis}
\end{equation}

With complex spherical harmonics, real-valued perturbations satisfy the usual
conjugacy constraints on the coefficients.  In a numerical implementation one
may instead use real spherical harmonics, in which case all coefficients are
real.

\subsection{Coefficient-space model induced by analysis}

Applying the analysis operators to the natural bulk and surface model spaces
gives the coefficient-space model used by the splitting-function formulation.
For each retained angular index \((s,t)\), define
\begin{equation}
    \modelspace_{st}
    =
    \modelspace^{v_p}_{st}
    \oplus
    \modelspace^{v_s}_{st}
    \oplus
    \modelspace^{\rho}_{st}
    \oplus
    \mathbb R^{N_\Sigma}.
    \label{eq:app_model_block}
\end{equation}
A model block is
\begin{equation}
    m_{st}
    =
    \left(
    \delta v_{p,st},
    \delta v_{s,st},
    \delta\rho_{st},
    \delta\Sigma_{1,st},\dots,\delta\Sigma_{N_\Sigma,st}
    \right).
    \label{eq:app_model_block_tuple}
\end{equation}
The reduced model space is
\begin{equation}
    \modelspace_{\mathcal I}
    =
    \bigoplus_{(s,t)\in\mathcal I}
    \modelspace_{st}.
    \label{eq:app_full_model_space}
\end{equation}

For the shear-wave perturbation, we account for the fluid outer core by
splitting the radial domain:
\begin{equation}
    \modelspace^{v_s}_{st}
    =
    \modelspace^{v_s,IC}_{st}
    \oplus
    \modelspace^{v_s,M}_{st},
    \label{eq:app_vs_split}
\end{equation}
reflecting the fact that \(v_s\) vanishes in the fluid outer core.

The coefficient-space inner products are induced by
\eqref{eq:app_3d_bulk_inner_product} and \eqref{eq:app_surface_inner_product}.
Using orthonormality of the spherical harmonics, the bulk inner product becomes
\begin{equation}
    \left(f,g\right)_{\Omega,p}
    =
    \sum_{(s,t)\in\mathcal I}
    \int_0^R
    w_p(r)\,
    f_{st}(r)\,
    g_{st}(r)
    \,dr
    \label{eq:app_bulk_inner_product_decomp}
\end{equation}
after restriction to the retained harmonic subspace.  Hence the induced radial
inner product is
\begin{equation}
    \left(f_{st},g_{st}\right)_{p,st}
    =
    \int_0^R
    w_p(r)\,
    f_{st}(r)\,
    g_{st}(r)
    \,dr.
    \label{eq:app_induced_radial_inner_product}
\end{equation}
Similarly, for the interface perturbation coefficients,
\begin{equation}
    \left(\delta\Sigma_\beta,\delta\widetilde\Sigma_\beta\right)_{\Sigma_\beta}
    =
    q_\beta
    \sum_{(s,t)\in\mathcal I}
    \delta\Sigma_{\beta,st}\,
    \delta\widetilde\Sigma_{\beta,st}.
    \label{eq:app_surface_inner_product_decomp}
\end{equation}

Therefore the direct-sum inner product on \(\modelspace_{\mathcal I}\) is
\begin{equation}
    \left(m,\tilde m\right)_{\modelspace_{\mathcal I}}
    =
    \sum_{(s,t)\in\mathcal I}
    \left(m_{st},\tilde m_{st}\right)_{\modelspace_{st}},
    \label{eq:app_full_inner_product}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \left(m_{st},\tilde m_{st}\right)_{\modelspace_{st}}
    &=
    \left(\delta v_{p,st},\delta\tilde v_{p,st}\right)_{v_p,st}
    +
    \left(\delta v_{s,st},\delta\tilde v_{s,st}\right)_{v_s,st}
    \\
    &\quad
    +
    \left(\delta\rho_{st},\delta\tilde\rho_{st}\right)_{\rho,st}
    \\
    &\quad
    +
    \sum_{\beta=1}^{N_\Sigma}
    q_\beta\,
    \delta\Sigma_{\beta,st}\,
    \delta\widetilde\Sigma_{\beta,st}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:app_block_inner_product}
\end{equation}
Thus the interface coefficients inherit a Euclidean metric when \(q_\beta=1\).
The radial inner products depend on the chosen bulk weights \(w_p(r)\), which
encode the model-space geometry for the radial coefficient fields.

\subsection{Splitting coefficients as linear data}

Let
\begin{equation}
    \alpha=(n,\ell)
\end{equation}
denote a normal-mode multiplet, and let \(\mathcal A\) denote the set of
multiplets included in the data set.  For a spherically symmetric reference
Earth, the first-order perturbation of the frequency splitting matrix for
multiplet \(\alpha=(n,\ell)\) can be written as
\begin{equation}
    H^\alpha_{mm'}
    =
    \sum_{s,t}
    \mathcal Q^\ell_{mm';st}\,
    c_{\alpha st},
    \qquad
    -\ell\le m,m'\le \ell ,
    \label{eq:app_split_matrix_expansion}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal Q^\ell_{mm';st}\) are known angular coupling coefficients and
\(c_{\alpha st}\) are splitting coefficients associated with perturbations of
the angular frequency \(\omega_\alpha\).  If kernels are originally computed for
perturbations of \(\omega_\alpha^2\), they are converted to angular-frequency
kernels by multiplication by \(1/(2\omega_\alpha)\).

For self-coupled splitting functions, the angular selection rules imply that
only certain harmonic degrees are visible for each multiplet.  In particular,
scalar isotropic structure contributes only to even degrees satisfying
\begin{equation}
    0\le s\le 2\ell.
    \label{eq:app_selection_rule}
\end{equation}
We denote by \(\mathcal I_\alpha\subseteq\mathcal I\) the set of harmonic
indices retained for multiplet \(\alpha\), and define the total data-index set
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal J
    =
    \{\,(\alpha,s,t):\alpha\in\mathcal A,\ (s,t)\in\mathcal I_\alpha\,\}.
    \label{eq:app_data_index_set}
\end{equation}
The data space is
\begin{equation}
    \dataspace
    \cong
    \mathbb R^{N_d},
    \qquad
    N_d=|\mathcal J|.
    \label{eq:app_data_space}
\end{equation}

In this appendix we take the data to be noisy estimates of the splitting
coefficients \(c_{\alpha st}\).  Thus the forward map is indexed by
\(i=(\alpha,s,t)\in\mathcal J\).

\subsection{Decoupled forward map under the splitting-function approximation}

The spherical-harmonic transform alone does not imply that the inverse problem
decouples in \((s,t)\).  Decoupling follows from the structure of the
splitting-function approximation.  Specifically, we assume:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item the reference Earth is spherically symmetric, non-rotating, and
    isotropic at the level used to compute the kernels;
    \item the data are splitting coefficients \(c_{\alpha st}\), not raw
    splitting-matrix entries;
    \item distinct multiplets are treated through their self-coupled splitting
    functions, so cross-coupling between different multiplets is neglected in
    this reduced example;
    \item the radial kernels have already incorporated the angular reduction
    and any normalization required to express the data as angular-frequency
    perturbations.
\end{enumerate}
Under these assumptions, each datum \(c_{\alpha st}\) depends only on the model
block \(m_{st}\).  The forward map has the form
\begin{align}
    [G(m)]_{\alpha st}
    &=
    \int_0^R
    K_{\alpha s}^{v_p}(r)\,
    \delta v_{p,st}(r)\,dr
    +
    \int_0^R
    K_{\alpha s}^{v_s}(r)\,
    \delta v_{s,st}(r)\,dr
    \notag\\
    &\quad
    +
    \int_0^R
    K_{\alpha s}^{\rho}(r)\,
    \delta\rho_{st}(r)\,dr
    +
    \sum_{\beta=1}^{N_\Sigma}
    K_{\alpha s}^{\Sigma_\beta}\,
    \delta\Sigma_{\beta,st}.
    \label{eq:app_splitting_forward}
\end{align}
Here \(K_{\alpha s}^{v_p}\), \(K_{\alpha s}^{v_s}\), and
\(K_{\alpha s}^{\rho}\) are radial splitting kernels, while
\(K_{\alpha s}^{\Sigma_\beta}\) are boundary kernels for perturbations of the
interfaces \(\Sigma_\beta\).

Equation \eqref{eq:app_splitting_forward} shows that the forward map is block
diagonal in \((s,t)\):
\begin{equation}
    G
    =
    \bigoplus_{(s,t)\in\mathcal I}
    G_{st},
    \label{eq:app_forward_direct_sum}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    G_{st}:\modelspace_{st}\to\dataspace_{st}
\end{equation}
maps the model block \(m_{st}\) to the set of splitting coefficients
\(\{c_{\alpha st}:\alpha\in\mathcal A,\ (s,t)\in\mathcal I_\alpha\}\).

\subsection{Observation model}

Let \(\bar m\in\modelspace_{\mathcal I}\) denote the true perturbation after
projection to the retained harmonic subspace.  The observed splitting
coefficients are modeled as
\begin{equation}
    \dt
    =
    G(\bar m)+\bm{\eta},
    \label{eq:app_observation_model}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    \bm{\eta}\sim\mathcal N(0,\Cd).
    \label{eq:app_noise_model}
\end{equation}
Here \(\bm{\eta}\) denotes observational noise, not interface topography.

If the estimated splitting coefficients are treated as independent across
\((s,t)\), then
\begin{equation}
    \Cd
    =
    \bigoplus_{(s,t)\in\mathcal I}
    {\Cd}_{st}.
    \label{eq:app_cd_block}
\end{equation}
If \(\Cd\) contains cross-covariances between different \((s,t)\), then the
likelihood couples the subproblems, even though the forward map itself has the
form \eqref{eq:app_forward_direct_sum}.

\subsection{Prior covariance and preservation of the \texorpdfstring{$(s,t)$}{(s,t)} decoupling}

In principle, the prior should be defined on the natural three-dimensional and
surface model space \(\modelspace_{\mathrm{3D}}\).  If
\(C_0^{\mathrm{3D}}\) is a covariance operator on that space, then the induced
coefficient-space covariance is
\begin{equation}
    C_{0,\mathcal I}
    =
    \Pi_{\mathcal I}
    C_0^{\mathrm{3D}}
    \pi_{\mathcal I},
    \label{eq:app_projected_covariance}
\end{equation}
where \(\Pi_{\mathcal I}\) denotes the combined bulk and surface analysis
operator, and \(\pi_{\mathcal I}\) denotes the corresponding synthesis
operator.

The harmonic transform itself does not make the prior independent in
\((s,t)\).  Independence is obtained only if the projected covariance is block
diagonal:
\begin{equation}
    C_{0,\mathcal I}
    =
    \bigoplus_{(s,t)\in\mathcal I}
    C_{0,st}.
    \label{eq:app_block_prior}
\end{equation}
For a Gaussian prior, this block diagonal structure is equivalent to
independence of the model blocks \(m_{st}\).

A rotationally invariant three-dimensional prior gives such a structure,
because spherical harmonics diagonalize the angular part of any operator that
commutes with rotations.  In the present numerical implementation, however, we
use a reduced but compatible construction: each spherical-harmonic coefficient
field receives a radial Bessel-type covariance, and degree dependence is
introduced through scalar weights.  For \(p\in\{v_p,v_s,\rho\}\), we take
\begin{equation}
    C_{0,st}^{p}
    =
    \tau_{p,s}^2
    C_r^p,
    \label{eq:app_radial_prior_block}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    C_r^p
    =
    \left(
    k_p^2 I+\alpha_p(-\Delta_r)
    \right)^{-q_p/2}
    \label{eq:app_radial_bessel_covariance}
\end{equation}
is a one-dimensional Bessel-type covariance operator on the relevant radial
interval, with prescribed boundary conditions.  The factor \(\tau_{p,s}^2\)
controls the prior power at angular degree \(s\).  Taking \(\tau_{p,s}\)
independent of \(t\) treats all orders \(t\) at fixed degree \(s\) equally.

For the interface perturbation coefficients we use
\begin{equation}
    C_{0,st}^{\Sigma}
    =
    \tau_{\Sigma,s}^2
    \mathbf C_{\Sigma},
    \label{eq:app_interface_prior_block}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf C_{\Sigma}\in\mathbb R^{N_\Sigma\times N_\Sigma}\) may encode
prior correlations between perturbations of different interfaces within the
same \((s,t)\) block.

Thus a typical block covariance is
\begin{equation}
    C_{0,st}
    =
    \begin{pmatrix}
    C_{0,st}^{v_p} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & C_{0,st}^{v_s} & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & C_{0,st}^{\rho} & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & C_{0,st}^{\Sigma}
    \end{pmatrix}.
    \label{eq:app_prior_block}
\end{equation}
This prior is not a full three-dimensional isotropic Bessel prior.  Rather, it
is a reduced spherical-harmonic prior built from one-dimensional radial
covariance operators.  It is compatible with the splitting-function
decomposition because it preserves the same \((s,t)\) block structure as the
forward map.

\subsection{Blockwise posterior}

If both the prior covariance and data covariance are block diagonal in
\((s,t)\), then the posterior factorizes:
\begin{equation}
    \mposterior
    =
    \bigotimes_{(s,t)\in\mathcal I}
    \mu_{st}^{\dt}.
    \label{eq:app_posterior_factorization}
\end{equation}
For each \((s,t)\), the block observation model is
\begin{equation}
    \dt_{st}
    =
    G_{st}(m_{st})
    +
    \bm{\eta}_{st},
    \qquad
    \bm{\eta}_{st}\sim\mathcal N(0,{\Cd}_{st}).
    \label{eq:app_block_observation_model}
\end{equation}
The posterior on \(\modelspace_{st}\) is Gaussian:
\begin{equation}
    \mu_{st}^{\dt}
    =
    \mathcal N(\widetilde m_{st},C_{st}^{\mathrm{post}}),
    \label{eq:app_block_posterior}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde m_{st}
    =
    m_{0,st}
    +
    C_{0,st}G_{st}^{*}
    \left(
    G_{st}C_{0,st}G_{st}^{*}
    +
    {\Cd}_{st}
    \right)^{-1}
    \left(
    \dt_{st}
    -
    G_{st}(m_{0,st})
    \right),
    \label{eq:app_block_posterior_mean}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    C_{st}^{\mathrm{post}}
    =
    C_{0,st}
    -
    C_{0,st}G_{st}^{*}
    \left(
    G_{st}C_{0,st}G_{st}^{*}
    +
    {\Cd}_{st}
    \right)^{-1}
    G_{st}C_{0,st}.
    \label{eq:app_block_posterior_cov}
\end{equation}

This is the precise sense in which the splitting-function PLI problem reduces
to independent radial inverse problems: the reduction follows from the
block-diagonal structure of the forward map, prior covariance, and data
covariance in the spherical-harmonic basis.

\subsection{Adjoint of the block forward operator}

The adjoint \(G_{st}^{*}\) depends on the induced inner product on
\(\modelspace_{st}\).  Let
\[
    \mathcal R_{p,st}:
    \modelspace^p_{st}\to(\modelspace^p_{st})^*
\]
denote the Riesz map associated with the radial inner product
\eqref{eq:app_induced_radial_inner_product}.  For a data vector
\(\mathbf a_{st}\in\dataspace_{st}\), the adjoint has components
\begin{align}
    [G_{st}^{*}\mathbf a_{st}]^{v_p}
    &=
    \sum_{\alpha:\,(\alpha,s,t)\in\mathcal J}
    [\mathbf a_{st}]_\alpha\,
    \mathcal R_{v_p,st}^{-1}
    K_{\alpha s}^{v_p},
    \label{eq:app_adjoint_vp}
    \\
    [G_{st}^{*}\mathbf a_{st}]^{v_s}
    &=
    \sum_{\alpha:\,(\alpha,s,t)\in\mathcal J}
    [\mathbf a_{st}]_\alpha\,
    \mathcal R_{v_s,st}^{-1}
    K_{\alpha s}^{v_s},
    \label{eq:app_adjoint_vs}
    \\
    [G_{st}^{*}\mathbf a_{st}]^{\rho}
    &=
    \sum_{\alpha:\,(\alpha,s,t)\in\mathcal J}
    [\mathbf a_{st}]_\alpha\,
    \mathcal R_{\rho,st}^{-1}
    K_{\alpha s}^{\rho}.
    \label{eq:app_adjoint_rho}
\end{align}
For the interface coefficients,
\begin{equation}
    [G_{st}^{*}\mathbf a_{st}]^{\Sigma_\beta}
    =
    q_\beta^{-1}
    \sum_{\alpha:\,(\alpha,s,t)\in\mathcal J}
    [\mathbf a_{st}]_\alpha\,
    K_{\alpha s}^{\Sigma_\beta}.
    \label{eq:app_adjoint_sigma}
\end{equation}
When \(q_\beta=1\), this is the standard Euclidean adjoint on the interface
coefficient block.

\subsection{Property operators from three-dimensional targets}

The property operator should also be understood as a projected version of a
three-dimensional or surface property.  Let
\[
    \Tau_{\mathrm{3D}}:
    \modelspace_{\mathrm{3D}}\to\propertyspace
\]
be a linear property map on the natural model space.  The corresponding
coefficient-space property map is
\begin{equation}
    \Tau_{\mathcal I}
    =
    \Tau_{\mathrm{3D}}\pi_{\mathcal I}.
    \label{eq:app_projected_property}
\end{equation}
Thus properties are not defined independently in coefficient space; they are
obtained by synthesizing the retained harmonic field and applying the desired
three-dimensional or surface functional.

For example, a bulk property of \(\delta v_s\) may have the form
\begin{equation}
    \Tau_{\mathrm{3D}}(m)
    =
    \int_0^R
    \int_{\mathbb{S}^2}
    T(r,\xi)\,
    \delta v_s(r,\xi)\,
    r^2\,d\xi\,dr.
    \label{eq:app_3d_bulk_property}
\end{equation}
Expanding
\begin{equation}
    T(r,\xi)
    =
    \sum_{(s,t)\in\mathcal I}
    T_{st}(r)Y_s^t(\xi),
\end{equation}
gives the coefficient-space expression
\begin{equation}
    \Tau_{\mathcal I}(m)
    =
    \sum_{(s,t)\in\mathcal I}
    \int_0^R
    T_{st}(r)\,
    \delta v_{s,st}(r)\,
    r^2\,dr.
    \label{eq:app_projected_bulk_property}
\end{equation}

Similarly, a localized CMB-perturbation average may be written as
\begin{equation}
    \Tau_{\mathrm{3D}}(m)
    =
    \int_{\mathbb{S}^2}
    B(\xi)\,
    \delta\Sigma_{\mathrm{CMB}}(\xi)
    \,d\xi.
    \label{eq:app_surface_property}
\end{equation}
If
\begin{equation}
    B(\xi)
    =
    \sum_{(s,t)\in\mathcal I}
    B_{st}Y_s^t(\xi),
\end{equation}
then
\begin{equation}
    \Tau_{\mathcal I}(m)
    =
    \sum_{(s,t)\in\mathcal I}
    B_{st}
    \delta\Sigma_{\mathrm{CMB},st}.
    \label{eq:app_projected_surface_property}
\end{equation}

The property operator may combine multiple \((s,t)\) blocks.  Therefore, even
when the posterior factorizes over \((s,t)\), a property posterior may involve
sums of contributions from several independent blocks.

The posterior property measure is
\begin{equation}
    \mu_{\propertyspace}
    =
    \Tau_{\mathcal I\#}\mposterior
    =
    \mathcal N(\pt,\Cp),
    \label{eq:app_property_pushforward}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    \pt
    =
    \Tau_{\mathcal I}(\widetilde m),
    \qquad
    \Cp
    =
    \Tau_{\mathcal I}
    C_{\modelspace}^{\mathrm{post}}
    \Tau_{\mathcal I}^{*}.
    \label{eq:app_property_mean_cov}
\end{equation}
